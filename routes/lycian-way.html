Why Visit

The Lycian Way

A waymarked footpath along the coast of ancient Lycia, laid out on Roman roads, old village paths and mule trails, linking some twenty-five remote historical sites between the Mediterranean and the mountains behind it.

The trail was researched, designed and waymarked in 1999 by Kate Clow, a British-Turkish historian, as Türkiye's first long-distance walking route. The aim was as much preservation as recreation: to identify and protect old roads that were falling out of use. It runs across the Tekke Peninsula, the land the ancient world called Lycia, where mountains rise steeply from a rocky shore and pine gives way to juniper and cedar as you climb. Underfoot it is often hard and stony, and the gradients are steep enough that the trail is not suitable for mountain bikes.

Almost nobody walks the whole thing. The route is modular by design, with most stages ending in a village that has road access, so the common approach is to pick a region, walk for three to six days, and take a dolmuş back. That is the way to think about it: not one 35-day expedition, but eight distinct regions with very different characters, from the busy day-hiking country above Ölüdeniz to three remote mountain days above Demre where you will need to camp.

The Trail West to East · Eight Regions

The Lycian Way, broken into eight regions.

Eight regions, west to east. Tap a photograph, the map will follow.

01

Fethiye to Letoon

The busiest and most walked part of the trail, and a fair sample of everything that follows: constant climbing and descending, villages perched above the sea, Lycian tombs, and coves you can swim in at the end of the day. This is where day hikers concentrate and where most people who only want a taste of the Lycian Way come. Waymarking is generally good, water is rarely a worry because you pass through settlements regularly, and pensions are plentiful. The one real planning decision comes after Alınca, where you choose between the higher, more rugged line through Yediburunlar or the alternative past the Sidyma ruins near Dodurga.

03

Kalkan to Kaş

The trail climbs away from the coast into quiet mountain country between two resort towns, passing through a handful of villages where you can find a bed, water and basic supplies. The Phellos ruins are the historical draw. There are no significant route variants to weigh up here, which makes it a straightforward stretch to plan, though the long day between Gökçeören and Çukurbağ is a hard one and some hikers take a dolmuş rather than tackle the steep drop into Kaş. Kaş itself is the natural rest day on the western half of the trail, and the one place along the route with an outdoor gear shop.

05

Demre to Finike

Three mountain days with no accommodation on the route, which means carrying a tent or arranging logistics in advance. This is the most remote part of the Lycian Way: steep, rugged, and genuinely committing. Springs and wells exist but can run dry, and while shepherds on the high pastures will often sell you water and snacks, that is not something to plan around. The rewards are the Byzantine ruins at Alakilise, the Belos remains, and long views with nobody else on the path. Demre itself holds the ancient city of Myra and the Church of St Nicholas. If you are not equipped to camp, skip the region by dolmuş rather than walking the coast road.

07

Çıralı to Göynük Yayla

Here the trail splits into two official variants and both are good. The inland line climbs past Beycik under the dramatic bulk of Tahtalı Dağı, the ancient Mount Olympos, and is the tougher of the two, with an optional side climb to the summit or a cable car up from the coast. The coastal variant hugs the shore through the Phaselis ruins near Tekirova before turning up into the mountains. Either way, do not miss the burning rocks of the Chimaera above Çıralı, best seen after dark. Plan beds carefully on the inland line, as some villages have very limited or no accommodation.

02

Letoon to Kalkan

A flat delta region under a sea of greenhouses, and the part of the trail hikers most often skip. What redeems it is the archaeology: three significant ancient cities in Letoon, Xanthos and Patara, long stretches walking on the line of a Roman aqueduct, and the Delikkemer aqueduct bridge, which is still largely intact. Patara's sand beach is a welcome change from pebble. Expect road walking, ongoing construction that occasionally blocks the path, and overgrown scrub in places. If you are short on days, this is the region to cut, and many take a dolmuş straight to Kalkan and see the ruins as a day trip instead.

04

Kaş to Demre

For many walkers this is the finest stretch on the trail, following the coastline almost the whole way past sunken ruins, empty coves and the fishing villages of Üçağız and Kaleköy beneath the castle at Simena. There is no major altitude to gain, but the ground is relentlessly rocky and uneven with endless small climbs, so it takes longer than the profile suggests. Water can be unreliable, so carry extra until you have confirmed the fountains are running, and flat ground for a tent is scarcer than it looks. Approaching Demre, the coastal line through Çayağzı past the Andriake ruins is the more rewarding of the two options.

06

Finike to Çıralı

Three quite different stretches in one region. The first, from Finike to Karaöz, is a long flat walk on tarmac past greenhouses, widely considered the least interesting part of the whole trail and skipped by a large share of hikers. What comes after more than compensates: the route rounds the Gelidonya peninsula, with a lighthouse at the point and small islands offshore, then climbs over a mountain to drop into Çıralı through the Olympos ruins, where there is an admission charge. Adrasan and Çıralı are both holiday towns with plenty of beds, though in high summer they fill completely.

08

Göynük Yayla to Geyikbayırı

The final region is high, wild and beautiful, and the descent through Göynük Canyon is a highlight of the entire route, though a demanding one that can involve wading when water levels are high in early spring. Beyond the canyon the walking gets rougher, the waymarking is less reliable than elsewhere, and overgrown scrub is a recurring nuisance. There are few ruins compared with the west, so the appeal here is purely the mountains. Many hikers start or finish at Göynük town, a short way off the trail with full amenities and transport to Antalya, or at Hısarçandır, which was the trail's eastern end until 2014. Today the official finish is Geyikbayırı.

Before You Go

Planning Essentials

The Lycian Way is not technically difficult, but it is physically demanding and the practical details decide whether a walk goes well. These are the six things worth settling before you book anything.

Season
When to Walk

The Culture Routes Society recommends February to May and September to November, and singles out February as often warmer and sunnier than March. Summer is simply too hot for long walks on exposed, stony ground with little shade. High mountain sections hold snow into spring, so the Tahtalı area and the Demre highlands should wait until mid-May or later.

Distance
How Long It Really Is

The official figure is now 760 km over 35 days, but you will still see 540 km and 29 days quoted widely, including in the metadata of the official site itself, and older guidebook editions listed shorter distances again. The number grew as the route was extended and rerouted, not because the trail moved. We measured the mapped trail ourselves from OpenStreetMap route data and got 632.8 km across 24,767 points, which includes the official variants as well as the main line. The practical lesson: plan by region and by days, not by the headline total.

Supply
Water

In the western regions you pass through settlements often enough that water is rarely an issue. It becomes a real planning constraint from Kaş eastward, and especially on the three mountain days between Demre and Finike, where springs and wells exist but can be dry. Carry more than you think you need until you have confirmed the fountains ahead are running.

Accommodation
Where to Sleep

Except for three high-level sections, you can find a bed every night in village houses, pensions or small hotels. That is what makes the trail unusually approachable for a long-distance route. The exception matters though: the Demre to Finike crossing has nothing on the route, and a few inland villages on the Çıralı to Göynük stretch have lost their only guesthouse.

Maps and GPX
Navigation

The route is marked with red and white blazes to the standard European convention, but paint fades on exposed sections and reroutes happen, most often where construction or fire has forced a change. Carry a GPS track as insurance. The Culture Routes Society maintains the authoritative route data and notes that the complete updated route appears on OpenStreetMap, so an OSM-based app is the most reliable digital option.

Transport
Getting There

The western trailhead at Ölüdeniz is about two hours from Dalaman airport. The eastern end at Geyikbayırı has daily buses to Antalya. In between, public transport along the coast is good, and because nearly every stage finishes in a village with road access, you can walk a section and dolmuş back to your base rather than committing to the full route.

If You Only Have a Few Days

The Best Short Sections

Most walkers never do the full trail. These are the stretches worth choosing if you have two days or a week

Ölüdeniz to Faralya
★★★★★ 1 day · moderate

The single most walked day on the trail and the easiest to reach if you are staying anywhere near Fethiye. Climbs out of Ölüdeniz onto the ridge above Butterfly Valley with the lagoon behind you and paragliders overhead. Dolmuş back from Faralya. If you want one day on the Lycian Way, this is it.

Best single day
Faralya to Kabak
★★★★★ 1 day · easy to moderate

Short, scenic and manageable for most fitness levels, linking two villages that both have good places to stay. Kabak's valley and beach make a natural overnight, and there is a longer coastal variant if you want more time on your feet.

Easy first taste
Kabak to Alınca
★★★★ 1 day · demanding

A hard, spectacular day climbing away from Kabak to the tiny settlement at Alınca, high above the sea with one of the best views on the entire route. Steep and rocky, with limited water. The reward is the sense of leaving the resort coast behind entirely.

The big view
Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy
★★★★★ 2 to 3 days · moderate

The most rewarding multi-day stretch for scenery. Follows the coast past sunken ruins and empty coves into the fishing villages of Üçağız and Kaleköy, with the castle at Simena above. Rocky underfoot and slower than it looks, but beds and boats are available throughout.

Best coastal walk
The Patara and Xanthos cluster
★★★★ 1 to 2 days · easy

Not the best walking, but the densest archaeology on the trail: three major ancient cities plus the Delikkemer aqueduct within a short radius, and Patara's sand beach to finish. Best treated as flat, easy days or even as day trips from a base in Kalkan.

For history
Adrasan to Çıralı via Olympos
★★★★★ 1 day · demanding

A mountain crossing between two holiday villages that finishes by walking straight through the ruins of Olympos, scattered through the trees along a stream. Combine it with the Chimaera flames above Çıralı after dark for the best evening on the trail.

Ruins and flames
Çıralı to Beycik under Tahtalı
★★★★ 2 days · demanding

Climbs inland beneath Tahtalı Dağı, the ancient Mount Olympos, on the tougher of the two official variants. There is an optional summit push, or you can take the cable car from the coast for the view without the climb. Check accommodation before you start.

High mountain
Göynük Canyon to Geyikbayırı
★★★★ 2 days · demanding

The finish, and a genuine highlight: a rugged descent through Göynük Canyon before the last mountain miles to the official trail end. Water levels can force wading in early spring, and waymarking is weaker here than in the west. Transport to Antalya from Göynük or Geyikbayırı.

Ruins on the Route

Ancient Cities on the Trail

01
Xanthos and Letoon

The political and religious heart of ancient Lycia, and jointly inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list. Xanthos holds the pillar tombs and theatre of the Lycian capital; Letoon, a short walk away, was its sanctuary. Both sit in the delta region, where the walking is dull but the archaeology is the densest on the whole route.

02
Patara

A major Lycian port and, by tradition, the birthplace of St Nicholas. The site spreads across dunes behind one of the longest sand beaches in Türkiye, with a restored bouleuterion, a theatre half-buried in sand and the remains of the lighthouse. The trail passes directly through.

03
Myra and St Nicholas

At Demre, the rock-cut tombs of Myra are carved into the cliff face above a Roman theatre, and the Church of St Nicholas in the town below marks where the historical bishop served. It is the natural rest point before or after the remote mountain crossing to Finike.

04
Simena and Kaleköy

A castle on a headland above a fishing village, reached on foot or by boat, with Lycian sarcophagi standing in the shallows offshore. Nearby Aperlai and the sunken ruins along this coast are the reason many walkers rate the Kaş to Demre stretch the finest on the trail.

05
Olympos and the Chimaera

The trail walks straight through Olympos, whose ruins lie scattered among trees along a stream running down to the beach. Above the neighbouring village of Çıralı, natural gas seeping from the rock has burned for millennia, the flames the ancients identified with the Chimaera. Go after dark.

06
Phaselis

Three harbours and a colonnaded main street set among pines right at the water's edge, on the coastal variant near Tekirova. One of the most pleasant sites on the route to arrive at on foot, and you can swim from the ancient harbour when you are done.

Going Further

Trails That Connect

From the eastern end · about 10 days
The St Paul Trail

Waymarking now links the Lycian Way's eastern end with the southern end of the St Paul Trail, which runs north from the coast towards Lake Eğirdir through the Taurus mountains. For anyone with a month or more, the two routes can be walked as one continuous journey inland from the Mediterranean.

West of Fethiye · 800 km
The Carian Trail

The Lycian Way's neighbour to the west, covering ancient Caria across the Bozburun and Datça peninsulas and the Muğla hinterland. Longer, quieter and less walked, it makes the natural next objective for anyone who finishes the Lycian Way and wants more of the same coastline without the crowds.

Common Questions

Lycian Way FAQ

How long is the Lycian Way?

The Culture Routes Society, which maintains the route, now describes it as 760 km. You will still widely see 540 km, and older guidebook editions listed shorter distances again. The figure grew because the route was extended and rerouted over the years, not because the trail was re-measured in one go. We measured the mapped route ourselves from OpenStreetMap data and got 632.8 km including the official variants. For planning, work in regions and days rather than the headline total.

How long does it take to walk the whole trail?

About 35 days for the full route at a normal walking pace. Very few people do this. The trail is built to be modular, with most stages ending in a village with road access, so the usual approach is to walk one region over three to six days and use public transport to get back.

When is the best time to walk the Lycian Way?

February to May and September to November. The Culture Routes Society specifically recommends February, which is often warmer and sunnier than March. Summer is too hot for long walks on this terrain. High sections near Tahtalı Dağı and above Demre can hold snow, so leave those until mid-May or later.

Is the Lycian Way difficult?

It is not technically difficult and requires no climbing skill, but it is physically demanding. The ground is hard and stony, the gradients are steep, and many stages involve several hundred metres of ascent. Ankle-supporting boots and trekking poles make a real difference on the descents.

Do you need a guide?

No. The route is waymarked with red and white blazes and most people walk it independently. That said, paint fades on exposed stretches and reroutes happen, so carry a GPS track as backup. First-time long-distance walkers sometimes prefer a guided or self-guided package for the remote sections.

Where does the Lycian Way start and end?

The western start is at Ölüdeniz near Fethiye, roughly two hours from Dalaman airport. The official eastern end is now Geyikbayırı, which has daily buses to Antalya. Until 2014 the eastern terminus was at Hısarçandır, which is why some older sources differ.

Is there accommodation, or do you have to camp?

Except for three high-level sections you can find a bed every night in village houses, pensions or small hotels, which is unusual for a long-distance trail. The main exception is the three-day mountain crossing between Demre and Finike, which has nothing on the route and requires a tent or arranged logistics.

Is water available on the trail?

In the western regions you pass settlements often enough that water is rarely a problem. From Kaş eastward it becomes a genuine planning issue, and on the Demre to Finike crossing springs and wells can be dry. Carry extra until you have confirmed the sources ahead are running.

What is the best short section if I only have a few days?

For one day, Ölüdeniz to Faralya above Butterfly Valley. For two or three days, the coastal stretch from Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy is the most rewarding walking on the route. For ruins and atmosphere, Adrasan to Çıralı through Olympos, finished with the Chimaera flames after dark.

Can you walk the Lycian Way solo?

Yes, and many people do. The route passes through villages regularly in most regions and public transport along the coast is good. For a first solo long walk, the busier western regions are the easier introduction; the remote mountain crossing between Demre and Finike is the one stretch to think carefully about going alone.
